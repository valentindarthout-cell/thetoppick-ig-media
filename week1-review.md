### D2-E  Educational  (carousel, 5 slides)  12:30

**Hook:** In a stock deal, the P/E gap decides accretion.

**Topic:** ma-accretion-dilution  |  **Source:** MA-101

**Slides:**
1. [M&A technicals] In a stock deal, the P/E gap decides accretion.
2. [The rule] Acquirer P/E above the target’s: accretive. / Below: dilutive. / Strictly, the target’s P/E at the offer price. All-stock deal, ignoring synergies and fees.
3. [Why it works (illustrative)] Acquirer / €2 EPS, €40 share price, 100 shares. P/E 20x. / Target / €50 of earnings, bought for €500. P/E 10x. / Payment / €500 in stock at €40 a share: 12.5 new shares. / Result / €250 of earnings over 112.5 shares: €2.22 EPS, up from €2.00.
4. [Flip it] Same deal, but the target costs 25x earnings. / €1,250 of stock at €40: 31.25 new shares. / €250 of earnings over 131.25 shares: €1.90 EPS. / Down from €2.00. Dilutive.
5. [What they listen for] Anyone can recite the rule. The interviewer is listening for why it works. / You issue shares priced at your own multiple to buy earnings priced at a lower one.

**Caption:**

> In an all-stock deal, accretion comes down to one comparison: the acquirer’s P/E against the P/E of the price paid for the target.
> 
> A higher acquirer P/E buys earnings cheaply relative to your own, so EPS rises. A lower one does the opposite.
> 
> Figures are illustrative and ignore synergies, fees and financing effects.
>
> #investmentbanking #IBinterview #MandA #financecareers #accretiondilution

### D3-E  Educational  (carousel, 5 slides)  12:30

**Hook:** In a DCF, the terminal value is usually most of the value.

**Topic:** valuation-dcf-terminal-value  |  **Source:** lead-magnet-fit-answers.md #15, VAL-189, VAL-177

**Slides:**
1. [Valuation] In a DCF, the terminal value is usually most of the value.
2. [Why] Five to ten years are forecast. Every year after that sits in one number. / For a going concern, that is a lot of years.
3. [Two ways to get it] Perpetuity growth / Cash flows grow at a constant rate forever, discounted as a perpetuity. / Exit multiple / Apply a market multiple, such as EV/EBITDA, to the final forecast year.
4. [What it means] Small changes in WACC or growth move the output a lot. / So analysts present a sensitivity table or a range, not one falsely precise number.
5. [The cross-check] Check one method against the other. / Work out the multiple your growth rate implies, and the growth rate your multiple implies. If either looks unreasonable, revisit the assumptions.

**Caption:**

> A DCF values a company on the present value of its future cash flows. The forecast covers five to ten years. The terminal value covers everything after, and it is usually most of the total.
> 
> That is why the output is so sensitive to two assumptions: the discount rate, and the growth rate or exit multiple.
> 
> Present a range, and cross-check one terminal value method against the other.
>
> #investmentbanking #IBinterview #MandA #financecareers #DCF

### D4-A  Answer Comparison  (carousel, 5 slides)  19:30

**Topic:** accounting-why-ebitda  |  **Source:** ACC-103, lead-magnet-fit-answers.md #13

1. [Real interview question] “Why do we use EBITDA rather than net income?”

**Caption:**

> “Why do we use EBITDA rather than net income?”
> 
> Average: it removes interest, tax and non-cash items. True, and one sentence too short.
> 
> A top pick explains what EBITDA is for: operating profit before the capital structure, the tax regime and accounting choices, which makes companies comparable and matches the capital-structure-neutral enterprise value.
> 
> Then shows where it misleads. It ignores capex. A telecom operator and a software firm can have the same net income and very different EBITDA, and EBITDA flatters the one that has to keep spending to maintain its assets.

### D5-E  Educational  (carousel, 5 slides)  12:30

**Hook:** The EV to equity bridge is not a formula. It is a ranking of claims.

**Topic:** valuation-ev-to-equity-bridge  |  **Source:** lead-magnet-fit-answers.md #14, VAL-211, VAL-220

**Slides:**
1. [Valuation] The EV to equity bridge is not a formula. It is a ranking of claims.
2. [What EV is] Enterprise value is the value of the operations to everyone who funds them.
3. [What comes off] Debt. / Preferred equity. / Minority interests. / Debt-like items, such as unfunded pensions. / Each ranks ahead of common shareholders, so each is removed.
4. [What goes on] Cash / It is not part of the operations. / Investments in associates / Their income is not in EBITDA. / Minorities come off because EBITDA is consolidated at 100%, but you own only part of it.
5. [The finish] Divide by fully diluted shares and you have a price per share. / Know why each item moves, not just which way.

**Caption:**

> Going from enterprise value to equity value is not a list to memorize. Every item has a reason.
> 
> Enterprise value is the value of the operations to all capital providers. Debt, preferred equity, minority interests and debt-like items such as unfunded pensions rank ahead of common shareholders, so they come off. Cash and investments in associates are not part of the operations, so they go on.
> 
> Then divide by fully diluted shares for a price per share.
>
> #investmentbanking #IBinterview #MandA #financecareers #valuation

### D6-E  Educational  (carousel, 5 slides)  12:30

**Hook:** A company with flat EBITDA can still return 1.5x in an LBO.

**Topic:** lbo-deleveraging-return  |  **Source:** CS-001, LBO-105

**Slides:**
1. [LBO] A company with flat EBITDA can still return 1.5x in an LBO.
2. [The setup (illustrative)] Buy at 8.0x on $100M of EBITDA: $800M. / Funded 70% debt, 30% equity: $560M of debt, $240M of equity.
3. [Three years later] $40M of free cash flow a year repays $120M of debt. / $440M of debt is left. / Sell at the same 8.0x: $800M. Equity value is $360M.

**Caption:**

> Deleveraging is one of the three LBO return levers, alongside EBITDA growth and multiple expansion. It is also the only one that works with a flat business.
> 
> Illustrative case: buy at 8.0x on $100M of EBITDA, funded 70% debt and 30% equity. Three years of $40M free cash flow repay $120M of debt. Sell at the same multiple, and $360M of equity value sits on $240M invested: 1.5x, roughly 15% a year.
